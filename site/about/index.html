One developer. One binary. One vision.

Stockyard started with a simple frustration: every AI project needed the same infrastructure — a proxy, a logger, a cost tracker, safety filters, an audit trail — and every team built it from scratch. Six tools, six bills, six points of failure.

So I put all of it in one Go binary. No Redis. No Postgres. No Docker compose. Just curl | sh and you're running.

That was the LLM proxy. But the proxy was just the door.

Behind it, I saw something bigger: an economy. A platform where anyone can build AI apps without code and publish them to a marketplace. Where GPU owners earn money from idle hardware by contributing compute to a distributed mesh. Where domain experts monetize their knowledge through a knowledge base marketplace with automatic royalties.

Today Stockyard is 16 integrated apps, 58 middleware modules, 350+ API endpoints, a declarative deployment engine, a natural language control interface, an AI incident response system, a distributed compute network, a knowledge marketplace, a financial layer, a governance framework, and an open protocol specification.

All in a single binary. All with zero external dependencies. All open source.

The mission

Make AI infrastructure accessible to everyone. Not just developers at well-funded companies. Everyone. The startup founder who needs to ship an AI feature today. The GPU owner who wants to earn from idle hardware. The retired doctor whose medical expertise could save lives if it were accessible to AI systems. The non-technical entrepreneur who has a great idea for an AI application but can't code.

The AI economy shouldn't require a DevOps team, a cloud budget, and six months of infrastructure work. It should require one binary and 30 seconds.

Open source

Stockyard is MIT licensed and fully open source. The entire codebase is at github.com/stockyard-dev/Stockyard. Read every line. Audit every decision. Fork it, extend it, contribute to it. The platform belongs to the community that uses it.

Built in the open

Every commit is public. Every decision is documented. The blog explains the engineering tradeoffs. The changelog tracks every ship. The protocol specification is published for anyone to adopt. Transparency isn't a marketing strategy. It's how trust is built.
